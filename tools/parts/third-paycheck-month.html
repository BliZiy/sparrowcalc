What to do with a 3rd paycheck in a biweekly month

Paid every two weeks? Twice a year, a month brings three paydays. Here's how to spot those months early and give that extra check a real job.

By SparrowCalc · Updated October 2026 · About 7 minutes

Why a biweekly schedule gives you "extra" paychecks

A biweekly schedule pays you every 14 days. A year has 52 weeks, so that works out to 26 paychecks. If you split those evenly across 12 months you'd get about 2.17 checks a month, but paychecks don't split. Most months hold exactly two paydays, and the leftover days build up until one month gets a third. In a normal year that happens twice. In some years, when January 1 (or January 2 in a leap year) falls on a payday, you get 27 paychecks and a third 3-paycheck month.

The important thing isn't the math. It's how most people budget. If your rent, utilities, groceries, and car payment are all planned around two paychecks a month, then the third check in those special months isn't needed for any regular bill. It's the closest thing to "found money" that a regular salary gives you. That's also why it disappears easily: it lands, nothing is waiting for it, and two weeks later it's gone on takeout and small purchases.

Step 1: Find your 3-paycheck months now

Which months get the extra check depends on your specific paydays, not just the calendar. Two coworkers paid on alternating Fridays can have completely different 3-paycheck months. Do this once at the start of the year and write the months on your calendar. Knowing in January that an extra check arrives in May and October changes how you plan. You can make decisions ahead of time instead of in the moment, when it's tempting to spend.

One caution: your employer's pay calendar always wins. If a payday falls on a bank holiday, it may move a day earlier, and occasionally that pushes it into a different month. Check your HR or payroll calendar if a payday sits right at the end of a month.

Step 2: Budget on two checks, not three

The whole idea only works if your regular monthly budget is built on two paychecks. If you currently budget with your average monthly income (annual pay divided by 12), the third check is already quietly spent. In most months you're coming up a little short, and the third check makes up the gap.

Switching to a two-paycheck budget can feel tight at first, because you're planning on less than your average income. But it makes the rest of the year easier. Each normal month is fully covered, and the 3-paycheck months become planned surpluses instead of patches over shortfalls. Step 3: Give the third check a job before it arrives

There's no single right answer for where extra money should go. It depends on your situation. Here's a practical order of priorities many people find useful, from most urgent to least.

1. Catch up on anything that's behind

If you have past-due bills, late fees building up, or an overdrawn account, the third check should fix that first. Getting back to zero stops the extra costs and lowers stress quickly. It's not exciting, but it pays off immediately.

2. Build or refill a starter emergency fund

A small cash buffer, often suggested as $500 to $1,000 to start, keeps a flat tire or a vet visit from turning into credit card debt. If you don't have one, one or two third checks can build it in a single year. Keep it somewhere separate from your everyday checking so it isn't spent by accident, such as a savings account at the same bank or a different one.

3. Make an extra debt payment

If you carry high-interest debt such as credit cards, an extra payment can make a real difference. A whole paycheck sent to your target debt can take months off your payoff timeline, and every dollar of principal you pay down early stops collecting interest. The snowball vs avalanche calculator lets you compare payoff plans, and our guide to debt snowball vs avalanche explains which debt to target first.

4. Fund your sinking funds

A sinking fund is money you set aside for an expense you know is coming but that doesn't arrive every month: car registration, holiday gifts, annual subscriptions, school supplies, insurance premiums, or a yearly membership. These irregular costs break more budgets than emergencies do, because they're easy to forget. A third check divided across a few sinking funds can cover most of a year's irregular costs.

5. Save for bigger goals

Once the basics are covered, the third check can go toward longer-term goals: a larger emergency fund (three to six months of expenses is a common target), a house down payment, a car replacement fund, or retirement contributions. If your employer offers a retirement match you aren't fully using yet, talk to HR or a qualified professional about adjusting your contribution instead of relying on one-off deposits.

6. Enjoy some of it, on purpose

A budget you hate is a budget you'll quit. Many people set a rule such as "80% goes to goals, 20% is fun money," so the third check feels like a reward without undoing their progress. Planning the fun part ahead is very different from letting the whole check slip away unplanned.

A simple example

Say your take-home pay is $1,400 per check, and the calculator shows your extra checks arrive in May and October. That's $2,800 a year your regular budget doesn't depend on. One plan could look like this:

  • May check ($1,400): $800 to finish a $1,000 starter emergency fund, $400 extra toward your smallest credit card, and $200 into a holiday sinking fund.
  • October check ($1,400): $1,000 to the credit card, $250 more to the holiday fund (now $450 before shopping season), and $150 for a planned weekend outing.

Nothing about this is extreme. The difference is that every dollar was assigned months before it arrived.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming the third check is a raise. Your annual income hasn't changed. It's just arriving in an uneven pattern.
  • Forgetting deductions. Some employers take benefit premiums or other deductions from only two checks a month, so the third check may be bigger than usual. Others take them from every check. Look at a pay stub instead of guessing.
  • Leaving it in checking. Money sitting next to your bill money tends to get spent. Move it to its assigned place the day it arrives.
  • Deciding on payday. Decide in advance. Payday is the worst time to make the call.

What about 27-paycheck years?

In a 27-pay year you get three 3-paycheck months instead of two. Some salaried employers keep your annual salary the same and divide it by 27, so each check is slightly smaller. Others pay the normal amount and you simply earn a bit more that year. If the calculator shows a 27-pay year coming, ask payroll how they handle it so your two-check budget stays accurate.

The bottom line

Two or three times a year, a biweekly schedule hands you a paycheck your bills don't need.
