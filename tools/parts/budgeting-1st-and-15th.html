How to budget when you're paid on the 1st and 15th

Semi-monthly pay looks simple: two checks a month, every month. The tricky part is that your bills don't follow the same schedule. Here's how to line them up.

By SparrowCalc · Updated October 2026 · About 7 minutes

Semi-monthly isn't the same as biweekly

Semi-monthly pay means two paychecks a month on fixed dates, usually the 1st and 15th or the 15th and last day. That's 24 checks a year. Biweekly pay means every other week, which works out to 26 checks a year and occasional months with three paydays. (If that's you, read what to do with a 3rd paycheck instead.)

Semi-monthly has one big advantage: it's predictable. Every month looks the same, so once you build a plan it works all year. Its main drawback is that each check is a bit larger than a biweekly check but there are fewer of them, and the gap between paydays can stretch to 16 or 17 days. If your biggest bills cluster at the start of the month, one check can carry far more than its share.

Step 1: Know your real take-home pay

Start with your net pay, the amount that actually reaches your bank account after taxes, insurance, and retirement contributions. Look at a recent pay stub rather than estimating from your salary. Some deductions, such as certain benefit premiums, may come out of only one of the two checks, so the 1st and 15th checks may not be exactly equal. Write down each one separately.

Also check what happens when a payday falls on a weekend or holiday. Most employers pay on the business day before, which means your "1st" check might arrive on the 30th or 31st of the previous month. That's helpful for rent, but it can confuse a monthly budget if you don't expect it.

Step 2: List every bill with its due date

Make a complete list of regular expenses: rent or mortgage, utilities, phone, internet, insurance, car payment, minimum debt payments, subscriptions, childcare, and so on. Next to each one, write the due date and the amount. Don't forget bills paid automatically, which are the easiest to miss.

Then add the flexible spending categories that don't have a due date but still need money every pay period: groceries, gas, household supplies, and personal spending. These are usually best split in half, with each check covering two weeks.

Step 3: Assign each bill to a paycheck

The simplest rule is:

  • Bills due from the 1st through the 14th are paid from the 1st check.
  • Bills due from the 15th through the end of the month are paid from the 15th check.

Step 4: Fix the lopsided check

Here's where most semi-monthly budgets run into trouble. Rent is often due on the 1st, so the 1st check carries the biggest bill of the month plus everything else due in the first two weeks. The 15th check might only have a phone bill and a streaming subscription. On paper the month balances. In real life, the first half of the month is a struggle and the second half feels loose, and that loose money tends to get spent.

A few ways to even things out:

  1. Save half of rent from the 15th check. Move part of the 15th check into a separate "rent" account or savings bucket, so the 1st check only needs to cover the other part. This is the most common fix and needs no one else's cooperation.
  2. Ask to move due dates. Many credit card issuers, utilities, and lenders let you change your due date. Moving a couple of bills from the 5th to the 20th can balance the checks.
  3. Pay early. A bill due on the 3rd can be paid from the 15th check of the month before, as long as you keep track. This gives you a buffer instead of paying exactly on the due date.
  4. Shift flexible spending. Give the lighter check a larger share of groceries or savings.

A simple example

Say you take home $1,600 on the 1st and $1,600 on the 15th. Your bills are rent $1,300 (due the 1st), car payment $320 (due the 10th), phone $60 (due the 18th), internet $70 (due the 22nd), and car insurance $140 (due the 25th). Groceries and gas run about $500 a month.

  • 1st check: rent $1,300 + car $320 + half of groceries/gas $250 = $1,870. That's $270 short.
  • 15th check: phone $60 + internet $70 + insurance $140 + groceries/gas $250 = $520. That's $1,080 left over.

The month as a whole has $810 left over, yet the first half doesn't work. The fix: from every 15th check, set aside $400 for next month's rent. Now the 1st check covers $1,470 and has $130 left, and the 15th check covers $920 and has $680 left for savings, debt, and spending. Same money, but no more scramble at the start of the month.

Step 5: Plan the leftover money

Whatever is left after bills and essentials should get a job too, or it will disappear. Common choices are an emergency fund, extra debt payments (see debt snowball vs avalanche), and sinking funds for irregular costs such as car repairs, gifts, and annual fees. A good habit is to move savings out on payday rather than waiting to see what's left at the end of the period.

Step 6: Review once a month

Semi-monthly budgets stay steady, but life doesn't. Spend ten minutes at the start of each month comparing planned vs actual spending, updating any bills that changed, and checking that each check still covers its share. Once a year, review your due dates and see if any could move to balance things better.

Common semi-monthly mistakes

  • Budgeting with gross pay. Always use take-home pay.
  • Forgetting annual and quarterly bills. Divide them by 24 and set that amount aside from each check.
  • Treating the 15th check as "spending money." If it looks big, it's usually because the 1st check is overloaded.
  • No buffer. Even $100 left in checking can absorb a bill that comes in slightly higher than expected.
